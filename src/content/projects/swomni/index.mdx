## Overview

**Swomni** (swerve + omni) is a drivetrain built from four independent pods. Each pod holds an **omni wheel**, a wheel with free-spinning rollers around its rim, and each pod can rotate about its vertical (yaw) axis to point in any direction.

That combination unlocks something a normal drivetrain can't do. When the robot is already moving forward and the wheels turn *inward*, the rollers start spinning to absorb the sideways motion, and the robot's forward speed climbs **past the motor's own top speed**. It behaves like a **continuously variable transmission (CVT)** built out of pure kinematics: no extra gears, just wheel angle.

My project had two goals: **prove the effect on real hardware**, and then find the **control strategy** that uses it best over short distances.

## The math

### Why tilting the wheels adds speed

Look at one wheel from above. The motor spins it at angular speed ω, so the rim moves at a fixed surface speed **ωr** along the direction the wheel points. The rollers on an omni wheel spin freely, so the wheel puts up no resistance to sliding *sideways*.

Now let the wheel sit at an angle θ from the direction the robot is travelling. The robot's velocity V splits into two parts: a part along the wheel's heading, which the motor must supply, and a sideways part that the rollers simply absorb.

<Tex block label="Velocity" expr="V\cos\theta = \omega r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{\omega r}{\cos\theta}" />

With θ = 0 the robot moves at the motor's normal speed ωr. As θ grows, cos θ shrinks and the achievable speed climbs; in theory it goes to infinity as θ approaches 90°. The four pods angle inward in mirror-image pairs, so their sideways components cancel and the robot still drives straight.

### The catch: traction

Speed isn't free. Each wheel pushes with force F along its own heading, and only the forward component of that push accelerates the robot:

<Tex block label="Force" expr="F_{\text{net}} = 4F\cos\theta" />

Multiply force by speed and the angle cancels out, so the power delivered stays the same:

<Tex block label="Power" expr="P = F_{\text{net}}\,V = 4F\cos\theta\cdot\frac{\omega r}{\cos\theta} = 4F\,\omega r" />

Force and speed trade off against each other at constant power. That's the definition of a transmission, and since θ can take any value it's a *continuously variable* one: the effective gear ratio is simply **1 / cos θ**.

The engineering question is therefore **when** to shift. Angle the wheels too early and the robot can't accelerate; too late and the run is almost over. Physical limits like roller friction and gear backlash cap the real speed-up well before the math does.

## Mechanical design

### A coaxial swerve pod

Each pod has to do two independent jobs at once: **spin the wheel** and **rotate the whole pod**, with nothing tangling as the pod turns endlessly. I solved this with a **coaxial** design, where both power paths share the pod's vertical axis.

Because the drive belt runs down the **center of the steering axis**, the motor and servo stay bolted to the chassis while the pod rotates freely underneath them. The bevel gear then turns the vertical drive shaft 90° to spin the wheel axle.

## Electronics & control

<Grid cols={2}>
<div>

### Hardware

- **REV Robotics Expansion Hub** driving four 12 V DC motors and four **35 kg·cm DS3225 servos**
- **Android phone** as the central controller
- **Motor encoder and accelerometer** to measure speed and acceleration in real time

</div>
<div>

### Software

- **Kinematics** that convert a desired "gear ratio" into matching pod angles for all four wheels
- A **velocity-based control strategy**: accelerate with wheels straight, then start angling the wheels once measured speed reaches a set percentage of the no-CVT top speed
- **Test programs** to verify the mechanical behavior and log velocity and acceleration for every run

</div>
</Grid>

<Callout title="The control idea">

The robot first accelerates normally, with full traction. Once its measured speed reaches a threshold (say 50% of the no-CVT top speed), the software gradually rotates the pods inward, shifting into a "taller gear" while there's still enough traction to keep accelerating. Choosing that threshold well is the whole optimization problem.

</Callout>

## Testing & results

### Experiment

In each trial the robot accelerated until it reached a set percentage of its no-CVT top speed, and then the software began angling the wheels. I swept that switch point from **0% to 100% in 10% steps**, ran **5 trials at each setting**, and timed runs over **12 ft and 18 ft**.

### What the data shows

- **The CVT effect is real.** As the wheels angled inward, the robot's top speed rose from **4.99 ft/s to 7.90 ft/s**, a **63% increase** over the motor's normal limit.
- **There's a sweet spot.** Starting the shift between **40% and 60%** of top speed gave the best times at both distances. The 12 ft run dropped from 2.65 s to 2.40 s (**9.4% faster**) and the 18 ft run from 4.16 s to 3.64 s (**12.5% faster**). The best overall threshold was about **45%**.
- **Two phases of motion.** The acceleration profiles show a fast first phase where the robot reaches its normal top speed, then a slower second phase where the CVT keeps adding speed. The shift works best right as the first phase ends, which is exactly why 40–60% wins.
- **Top speed peaks around 81° of wheel angle** before roller friction takes over and speed falls off.

### Next steps

Future work would test **nonlinear shift schedules** (angling the wheels along a curve instead of a fixed threshold), plus multi-directional motion and outside factors like floor surface. The same idea could apply anywhere fast, efficient motion matters: competitive robotics, autonomous vehicles and industrial automation.
