## Overview

A **swerve drive** lets every wheel steer independently, so a robot can drive in any direction while facing any way. In FTC, swerve is already one of the hardest drivetrains to build. We added a twist: **each wheel module can also move up and down** on its own suspension, so the robot can drive over rough terrain and obstacles without losing traction.

Every module has one motor to spin the wheel and one servo to steer it. The challenge is fitting a coaxial gearbox, a steering bearing, and a suspension that still passes drive power into **the same small footprint**, and then making four of them run smoothly together.

## 3 DOF Power transmission

The heart of this design is how power reaches the wheel. Both the motor and the servo are **mounted to the chassis and never move**, yet the module under them can spin endlessly and slide up and down. That's only possible because everything is **coaxial**, sharing the module's vertical axis. Each module has three power paths:

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<Figure src="suspension-swerve/motor-geartrain.png" variant="rounded" alt="CAD close-up of the motor drive path: motor, belt to a vertical shaft, down to the wheel through bevel gears, with other parts shown transparent" caption="Drive path, highlighted in CAD" />
<div>

<PowerPath paths={[

The motor drives a timing belt to a central shaft, followed by a pair of spur gears. Those spur gears **double as a planetary gearset**, so the wheel keeps getting power however the module is turned. A bevel gear at the bottom turns the drive 90° into the wheel axle.

</div>
</Grid>

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<div>

<PowerPath start={2} paths={[
  { name: "Steer: turns the module", steps: ["Servo", "Timing belt pulley", "Module ring", "Large bearing"] }
]} />

The whole module is constrained inside **one large bearing** and turned by the servo through a belted pulley. Because the servo and motor stay fixed to the frame, the wheel can **rotate continuously** with no end stops and no wires twisting.

</div>
<Figure src="suspension-swerve/servo-geartrain.png" variant="rounded" alt="CAD close-up of the steering path: servo with an orange gear driving the large module ring gear" caption="Steer path, highlighted in CAD" />
</Grid>

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<Figure src="suspension-swerve/pod-cad.png" variant="plate" ratio="4/5" alt="CAD render of a single module: large ring gear on top, four linear rails with springs around a central shaft, and the wheel below" caption="One module: ring gear, rails, springs and wheel" />
<div>

<PowerPath start={3} paths={[

- The second spur gear carries a **bevel gear that is slip-fit on its shaft**, so it slides up and down while still passing drive power to the wheel
- **Four MGN7 linear rails** constrain the wheel so it can only move vertically, with no rocking
- **Three compression springs** carry the robot's weight and soak up bumps, giving **2.5″ of travel**

</div>
</Grid>

## Electronics & software

- **MT6701 analog absolute encoder** on every module to measure the wheel's steering angle directly
- **Two servo power injectors** to raise the voltage supplied to the steering servos
- Programmed in **Java**, using **second-order swerve kinematics** to reduce drivetrain skew when the robot drives and turns at the same time

## Build process

We 3D-printed prototypes to test the module design before finalizing the CAD, then assembled the modules with aluminum plates. After the first build came months of tuning: **gear spacing** to cut friction, **control software** to reduce driver lag, and getting all of the components to work together smoothly.
