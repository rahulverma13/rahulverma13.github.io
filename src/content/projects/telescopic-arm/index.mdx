## Overview

This arm is the main scoring mechanism of an FTC competition robot, and the most complex part on it. It does two things at once: it **extends** like a telescope and it **pivots** up and down, with both motions driven coaxially from motors mounted low on the robot.

Instead of buying linear slides, I designed a **boxtube arm**: four nested, pocketed aluminum tubes from **2″ × 2″ down to 0.5″ × 0.5″**. Custom bearing inserts guide each stage, and cascade-strung cord extends all four stages at once.

The hard part was making it **rigid**. Nested square tubes want to wobble, so most of the work was a long iteration loop on the inserts. The other hard part was **control**: an arm whose length changes also changes its center of mass and moment of inertia, which breaks a plain PID loop. I solved that with a gain-scheduled feedforward controller that adapts to the arm's pose in real time.

## Design

### Key features

- **Four pocketed aluminum tubes**, 2″ × 2″ down to 0.5″ × 0.5″, with isogrid pocketing to cut weight
- **Four-directional support** at every stage from bearing-based inserts, inside and outside each tube
- **Cascade-strung extension** with four total strings, pulling every stage out together from a 36 mm / 12 mm spool
- **32 mm aluminum tube pivot axle**, with extension and pivot powered coaxially
- **Aluminum inserts integrated with 3D-printed parts** where the loads are highest, plus wiring zip-tie mounts
- **4× stiffer** and **47% lighter** than linear slides (395 g vs. 758 g), in a compact **2″ × 2″** footprint

### Build

The build combines laser-cut, pocketed aluminum tubing, aluminum insert plates and dozens of 3D-printed parts, all held to tight tolerances. Every tube is guided by bearings on all four sides, so the stages slide smoothly without rattling.

Stringing a four-stage cascade is its own puzzle. Each stage's string has to be routed and tied off so every tube extends and retracts in sync, with printed tie-off points built into the inserts.

The design carried real risk, so I reduced it up front: I planned the stringing carefully, kept the pocketing accessible for assembly, and got CAD feedback from FTC team #5064, who have built boxtube mechanisms before.

## Iteration

Every stage of the arm rides on custom **bearing inserts**: an *inside* insert fixed to the bottom of each inner tube, and an *outside* insert at the mouth of each outer tube. Getting them right took **43 iterations**, balancing two goals that fight each other: rigidity (tight fit) and smooth, low-friction sliding (loose fit).

### Inside bearing inserts

<Figure src="telescopic-arm/inside-inserts.png" variant="plate" bleed="top" alt="Three pink inside bearing inserts at the base of the 0.5, 1 and 1.5 inch tubes, each with bearings on all four sides" caption="Final inside inserts for the 0.5″, 1″ and 1.5″ tubes" />

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<Figure src="telescopic-arm/inside-v1-v2.png" variant="plate" alt="Version 1 gray insert with small side bearings next to version 2 pink insert with larger bearings" caption="V1 → V2" />

- **V1:** small bearings (2.5 mm wide, 6 mm OD) gave low contact area; the screw was hard to tighten and thin walls around the rods could break
- **V2:** larger bearings (4 mm wide, 8 mm OD) for more contact area, a nut capture for the screw, and more printed material around the rods

### Outside bearing inserts

<Figure src="telescopic-arm/outer-inserts.png" variant="plate" bleed="bottom" alt="Three pink outside bearing inserts clamped around tube ends, each with rows of bearings on rods" caption="Final outside inserts: 19 bearings give four-directional support" />

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<Figure src="telescopic-arm/outer-v1-v2.png" variant="plate" bleed="bottom" alt="Side-by-side close-ups of V1 and V2 outside inserts with the side bearing circled in red; V2 has a larger bearing and aluminum-backed plate" caption="V1 (left) vs. V2 (right): larger side bearings, aluminum backing" />
<div>

- **V1** was easy to make, but side bearings broke, the 3D-printed body flexed, and the arm had **31 mm of slop** at full extension
- **V2** uses **12 mm side bearings** held by dowel pins, an **aluminum-backed inner plate** with tapped holes for the printed parts, and swappable plates for tuning the fit: **5 mm max slop**

</div>
</Grid>

### Tolerancing

<Grid cols={2} align="center">
<Figure src="telescopic-arm/tolerance-section.png" variant="plate" bleed="both" alt="Section view of an insert inside a tube with the bearing-to-wall contact points circled in red" caption="Bearing-to-wall contact, the fit being tuned" />
<div>

Insert clearances were fine-tuned to a precision of **0.01 mm**, so each stage slides freely with almost no play. Inside and outside inserts each have their own clearance for every tube size, and the outside inserts are tuned separately on their side and front faces. Swappable aluminum plates made it possible to dial in each fit without reprinting the whole insert.

</div>
</Grid>

## Control

### The problem

Because the arm both pivots and extends, its **center of mass and moment of inertia change constantly**. A PID loop tuned for a short arm oscillates when the arm is long, and one tuned for a long arm is sluggish when it's short. With a single fixed PID, our cycles were unstable.

### Gain-scheduled feedforward

My fix was to **predict the load before correcting for error**. Using the pivot's absolute encoder (angle θ) and the extension motor's encoder (extension D), the controller computes the torque needed to hold the arm still in its current pose and feeds that in directly. PID then only has to clean up small errors. Drag the arm below to see how the load changes.

<ArmExplorer />

From a calculus-based model of the arm's mass distribution, the feedforward terms come out to:

<Grid cols={2}>
<Tex block label="Pivot" expr="\tau_{\text{pivot}}(\theta, D) = \alpha D g\cos\theta" />
<Tex block label="Extension" expr="F_{\text{ext}}(\theta) = m g\sin\theta" />
</Grid>

The pivot power scales with extension and the cosine of the angle; the extension power scales with the sine of the angle. Both are recomputed every loop from the encoders.

<Callout title="Result">

With feedforward carrying the gravity load, the arm behaves the same at every angle and extension. It reaches **full extension in 0.4 s** and pivots **90° in 0.25 s**.

</Callout>
